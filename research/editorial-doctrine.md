# The Window Brief — Editorial Doctrine

Status: mandatory guardrail for all public content  
Market focus: Western Australia first, Australia-ready  
Primary audience: Australian homeowners, renovators, architects and builders

## 1. Core identity

The Window Brief is not a European sales catalogue translated into Australian English.

It is an Australian-facing expert guide for people considering better windows and doors for Australian homes.

Primary promise:
> Better windows for Western Australian homes.

Core philosophy:
> Australian homes first. European technology where it makes sense.

Emotional reassurance:
> The technology can be European. The home stays Australian.

Supporting line:
> Still your Australian home. Just better to live in.

## 2. Cultural rule

Never frame the subject as:
- Europe teaching Australia how to build
- European windows being inherently superior
- Australian construction being backward
- imported products replacing Australian identity

Instead:
- respect Australian architecture and lifestyle
- preserve Federation, Victorian, Inter-War, California Bungalow, coastal and contemporary character
- begin with the home and the owner's lived experience
- show European engineering as one additional toolbox
- let local climate, site and project requirements decide whether a solution fits

## 3. The reader journey

### FEEL
Start with lived experience:
- too hot
- too cold near the glass
- too much noise
- draughts
- security concerns
- desire for larger openings
- desire to preserve the character of the home

### UNDERSTAND
Explain why the problem happens in plain Australian English.

### DISCOVER
Introduce alternative window and door engineering only after the problem is understood.

### VERIFY
Bring the solution back to Western Australia:
- Climate Zone 5 where applicable
- solar exposure and shading
- bushfire / BAL where applicable
- coastal corrosion
- wind and weather exposure
- aircraft and traffic noise
- flyscreens and security screens
- Australian glazing and window requirements
- local installation details
- Australian architecture and colour language

### CHOOSE
Help the reader understand what specification direction makes sense.

### ASK
Invite the reader to discuss the actual property or project.

## 4. Western Australia first

The first market focus is Western Australia because the project has a real local pathway for customer support, logistics and delivery.

Content should therefore feel locally useful before it feels nationally broad.

Useful WA contexts include:
- Perth / Fremantle metropolitan homes
- coastal exposure
- bushfire-prone properties
- airport and transport noise
- strong summer sun
- warm-temperate climate conditions
- large openings and indoor-outdoor living
- character homes and renovations

Do not imply that every WA property has every risk.
Always explain that site conditions vary.

## 5. Sales rule

The site must earn the enquiry before asking for it.

Do not lead with:
- Request a quote
- Buy European windows
- Contact our sales team
- Imported direct from Europe

Prefer:
- Ask about your home
- Tell me about your project
- Send your plans
- Not sure what makes sense? Ask.
- Start your Window Brief

The commercial pathway appears only after the expert value is clear.

Appropriate final-stage wording:
> If a suitable solution emerges, we can also help you understand the available supply and local support pathway.

Never disguise a commercial relationship. Where a referral, supplier or partner relationship is commercially relevant, disclose it clearly and calmly.

## 6. Expert positioning

The expert role is:
- technical guide
- project interpreter
- product/system adviser
- bridge between European manufacturing and Australian project requirements

Do not present the expert as:
- an Australian certifier
- a building surveyor
- an engineer
- the authority that determines statutory compliance

Where certification or local professional sign-off is required, state that clearly.

## 7. Tone of voice

Use:
- calm
- direct
- practical
- technically credible
- modest
- Australian English
- plain language first, technical language second

Avoid:
- hype
- superiority language
- exaggerated claims
- "revolutionary", "ultimate", "best in class", "unmatched"
- fake Australian slang
- overly German/European technical voice
- US spelling or US sales language

The ideal reaction is:
> This person understands the kind of house I live in.

Not:
> This person is trying to sell me European windows.

## 8. Page-writing sequence

Most pages should follow this order:

1. Australian situation
2. homeowner problem
3. short answer
4. why it happens
5. possible solutions
6. European engineering option
7. Western Australian reality check
8. real technical example
9. what changes from project to project
10. soft expert CTA

## 9. CTA ladder

### Level 1 — low friction
- Explore the options
- See how it works
- Compare approaches

### Level 2 — expert invitation
- Ask a question
- Not sure what suits your home?
- Send a photo or plan

### Level 3 — project CTA
- Tell me about your project
- Start your Window Brief

### Level 4 — commercial pathway
Only after needs are understood:
- recommended system direction
- suitable supplier or distribution channel
- local support pathway
- quotation or next commercial step where appropriate

## 10. Heritage and identity

A high-performance window should not erase the architectural identity of the house.

Core message:
> Keep the character. Upgrade the performance.

Examples:
- Federation homes should still read as Federation
- heritage proportions should be respected
- glazing bars, frame sightlines and colours matter
- traditional appearance and modern hidden performance can coexist

## 11. Product rule

The product is evidence, not the hero.

A page may use real systems such as SALAMANDER or ALUPROF to demonstrate:
- frame geometry
- glazing capacity
- hardware
- seals
- lift-and-slide construction
- thresholds

But the page should begin with the Australian project need, not the manufacturer's model number.

## 12. Final editorial test

Before publishing any page, ask:

1. Does this sound like it was written for an Australian home?
2. Is Australia the context rather than the audience being lectured?
3. Does the page respect local architecture and lifestyle?
4. Is Europe introduced as a toolbox rather than a superior culture?
5. Is the technical claim proportionate and supportable?
6. Is local compliance kept distinct from European product data?
7. Does the reader learn something useful before being asked to enquire?
8. Would a homeowner in WA feel that their house remains Australian?
9. Is the CTA expert-first and sales-second?
10. Could this page still be useful even if the reader never buys from us?

If the answer to any of these is no, revise the page.
